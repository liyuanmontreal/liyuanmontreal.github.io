Narrative Structure and Nonlinear Systems

I made an interesting discovery the day before yesterday: narrative structure is actually a nonlinear system.  

First, a question came to my mind: why is the density of good and bad characters in TV dramas and novels much higher than in real life? If we draw a straight line from good to bad, then people in real life roughly follow a Gaussian distribution, meaning that the majority are ordinary people in the middle, while extremely kind or extremely vicious people at the extremes make up only a small fraction. However, if we observe the distribution of characters in movies, TV dramas, and novels, many characters fall into either the very kind or very vicious range. They appear far more frequently than you would encounter such people in real life. Why? The answer is: From the perspective of node distribution in numerical analysis, the distribution of people you encounter in real life conforms to uniform sampling, while the characters in novels and television dramas conform to Chebyshev sampling. This method choose fewer nodes in the middle and denser nodes at both ends, making it particularly suitable for nonlinear systems.  

This raises another question: Why do novels and plays employ this sampling method? This is because narrative structure is essentially a non-linear system. Drama serves as a psychological compensation for real life. It inevitably carries people's life ideals and condenses their life experiences, thus presenting a dramatic quality that differs from reality. It is more condensed, with sharper contradictions and more stark contrasts than real life. Linear systems are characterized by smooth and uniform changes. Non-linear systems, however, are highly sensitive at certain points; small perturbations can produce enormous results.The stock market is a typical example of a non-linear system. It undergoes dramatic changes at certain points in time. The reason we use Chebyshev sampling to process the stock market is to capture these boundary changes. Narrative works are similar. A small misunderstanding can lead to a tragedy. A small object can trigger a bloodbath. Dramatic works must condense life experiences into a few dozen pages or two hours. Therefore, it is inevitable to present dramatic conflicts as much as possible, hence the need to collect as many of these dramatically changing boundary points as possible.  

This discussion can continue. What happens if we use uniform sampling for storytelling? The story becomes flat and monotonous, slow and stagnant, characters lack distinctiveness, and there's a lot of unmotivated and meaningless dialogue and plot. This corresponds to optimization problems where sampling too much of the middle part is meaningless because it fails to capture insightful changes, wastes a lot of computation, fails to learn key behaviors, and fails to converge.  

We can view narrative as a "non-linear model that can be understood by human emotions" constructed by humans on the complex reality. The real world is a high-dimensional, multi-factor system, encompassing various social structures, human nature, economics, opportunities, history, personality, and psychology. In narrative, this is condensed into several time periods, several characters, and several emotional threads. This is a projection of a high-dimensional world onto a low-dimensional world. As the plot develops, characters' personalities mostly change steadily, but when the plot touches a character's sensitive point (high-gain area), the character may suddenly change, break down, or awaken. This is a state jump that occurs at a high-gain event node.    

In summary, the narrative is actually constructed through Chebyshev-style node distribution, dense sampling of boundaries, nonlinear amplification, high-gain event nodes, state jumps, and dimensionality reduction, ultimately creating a small nonlinear system that is more aesthetically pleasing than the real world.
